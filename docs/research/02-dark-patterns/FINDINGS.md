# Dark Patterns / Deceptive Interface Design — and Their Transfer to Autonomous Agents

## Summary of what we actually established

1. Dark patterns are a mature field with a well-developed vocabulary (Brignull's ~12-item taxonomy from 2010, Gray et al.'s CHI 2018 academic taxonomy, Mathur et al.'s CSCW 2019 empirical 7-category/15-type scheme, and Gray/Santos/Bielova's 2023 three-level ontology synthesizing ten prior taxonomies). We should reuse this vocabulary rather than inventing our own category names. [DP-01][DP-02][DP-03][DP-06]
2. Large-scale crawls exist and give real prevalence numbers. Mathur et al. crawled ~11,000 shopping sites / ~53,000 product pages and catalogued 1,818 dark-pattern instances of 15 types; Nouwens et al. separately found dark-pattern cookie-consent designs on most of a 10,000-site UK sample. [DP-01][DP-09]
3. Regulators have converged on largely the same taxonomy: the FTC's 2022 staff report groups harms into four buckets (disguised ads, hard-to-cancel subscriptions, buried terms/junk fees, data-sharing tricks); the EU Digital Services Act (Article 25) legally bans interfaces that "deceive... manipulate... or materially distort or impair" free and informed choice, naming fake urgency/countdowns and obstructed cancellation explicitly. [DP-07][DP-08]
4. Directly on our critical angle -- agent transfer -- two 2025/2026 papers now exist and give hard numbers, not just plausible reasoning:
   - Cuvin, Zhu & Yang (Stanford), DECEPTICON (arXiv:2512.22894): dark patterns steer LLM web-agent trajectories to malicious/unintended outcomes in over 70% of 700 tested tasks, vs. a 31% human baseline on the same tasks -- agents are roughly 2.3x more susceptible than humans. Susceptibility increases with model size and test-time reasoning budget (larger/more-reasoning models are more fooled, not less). In-context prompting defenses and guardrail models "fail to consistently reduce" attack success. [DP-10]
   - Ersoy et al. (Purdue), Investigating the Impact of Dark Patterns on LLM-Based Web Agents (arXiv:2510.18113, IEEE S&P 2026): across six generalist web agents over three LLMs, agents fall for an individual dark pattern ~41% of the time on average; both the visual rendering and the underlying HTML/DOM encoding of a pattern independently move agent susceptibility, and stacking multiple dark patterns compounds the effect. [DP-11]
   - This closes a gap that existed as recently as early 2025: there is now direct, measured evidence that dark patterns transfer to agents, at high rates, and that naive prompt-level defenses do not fix it. This should anchor our threat model rather than our own speculation.
5. Automated detection of dark patterns is an active but noisy sub-field. Detectors split cleanly along the same axis that matters to us:
   - Text/DOM-structure classifiers (Mathur et al.'s crawler pipeline): ~83% accuracy (5-fold CV) on page text, cheap to run, no rendering required. [DP-12]
   - Screenshot/computer-vision classifiers (AidUI, UIGuard): must render and rasterize the page; AidUI reports overall P/R/F1 ~= 0.66/0.67/0.65 (much higher, >0.82, for visually distinctive patterns like countdown timers); UIGuard improves on this with P/R/F1 ~= 0.82-0.83/0.77-0.82/0.79-0.82 across 14 subtypes on 1,660 annotated screens. [DP-13][DP-14] Both of these numbers are secondary-source (WebSearch-snippet) and were not independently re-fetched -- treat as medium/low confidence pending direct PDF review.

## Per-pattern assessment: structurally detectable vs. requires semantics

This is the load-bearing table for the MCP proxy design. "Structural" means detectable from DOM/computed-style/geometry/form-state alone, without understanding the meaning of any text.

**Clickjacking / overlay interception**
- Structural mechanism: transparent/near-transparent element or cross-origin iframe stacked at a higher z-index over a decoy target; or an opaque overlay shown/hidden in a tight window around the click event.
- Prevalence: not separately quantified in the sources reviewed here (this is classic web-security literature, not a dark-pattern crawl finding). [DP-15] (unverified, secondary blog, low confidence)
- Detectability: fully structural. Detectable via computed z-index/opacity/pointer-events, bounding-box overlap of the hit-target vs. the visually-topmost element at the same coordinates (an elementFromPoint-style check), and cross-origin iframe presence. No text understanding needed.
- Agent-transfer note: likely worse for agents -- an agent that reads the DOM/accessibility tree and issues a coordinate or element-id click may never notice that the rendered topmost hit-target differs from the "intended" element, whereas a human visually notices something is off. Ersoy et al. show DOM-only changes to a pattern (independent of visual rendering) already move agent susceptibility. [DP-11] This is inference by analogy, not a direct finding -- no source specifically tested overlay-clickjacking against agents.

**Pre-selected/pre-checked options** (opt-outs pre-ticked, add-ons pre-added to cart)
- Structural mechanism: the "checked" attribute or a default value set server-side/client-side without user action; form state readable via element.checked / element.value.
- Prevalence: Nouwens et al. found dark-pattern consent designs (including pre-ticked boxes) present on most of a 10K-site sample. [DP-09] (unverified snippet)
- Detectability: fully structural. Checkbox/radio state is a boolean DOM property -- trivially queryable, no rendering or NLP required.
- Agent-transfer note: plausibly worse for agents, since an agent that fills a form field-by-field and does not diff "state before I touched the page" vs. "state as delivered" will silently submit the pre-set value. This is inference; DECEPTICON/Ersoy test dark patterns broadly and report aggregate 41%/70%+ manipulation rates, but neither source isolates pre-checked-box-only agent susceptibility as a standalone number in what was fetched here. [DP-10][DP-11]

**Hidden costs / drip pricing**
- Structural mechanism: fees injected at a later checkout step via a DOM update, or shown in a visually de-emphasized/collapsed element (small font, low contrast, display:none until a late step).
- Prevalence: Luguri & Strahilevitz found "hidden information" manipulation raised signup acceptance from 14.8% to 30.1% in a controlled experiment. [DP-05] Mathur et al. count "Sneaking" (which includes hidden costs) as one of their 7 top-level categories, drawn from real sites. [DP-01]
- Detectability: partially structural. The existence of a late-appearing cost node is structurally detectable (DOM diff across checkout steps, or CSS that hides/shrinks a price element). But judging that a cost was "hidden" versus reasonably disclosed requires some semantic/visual-hierarchy judgment -- not purely a DOM boolean.
- Agent-transfer note: direct agent evidence is aggregate-only (DECEPTICON/Ersoy show broad manipulability) rather than isolated to drip pricing specifically. Treat the "harder for agents" claim as inference: an agent that parses total price only at the end of a flow, and does not track price deltas per step, would not "notice" drip pricing the way a suspicious human might; conversely, an agent that dutifully reads the final total may be more robust than a human who stops re-reading. Genuinely uncertain -- flagged as an open question, not a finding.

**Obstructed cancellation ("roach motel")**
- Structural mechanism: cancellation flow requires more steps/clicks than signup (measurable via elements-per-nested-page or a forced synchronous "call us" requirement); e.g. cancel link removed from account settings, or gated behind extra confirm screens.
- Prevalence: Luguri & Strahilevitz found "obstruction" (extra steps to decline) raised acceptance to 23.6% vs. baseline. [DP-05] The FTC's 2022 report names hard-to-cancel subscriptions as one of its four core dark-pattern harm categories and has since brought enforcement actions on it. [DP-07] (unverified snippet)
- Detectability: semi-structural. Step-count asymmetry between signup and cancel flows is measurable (crawl depth, number of DOM state transitions) if you can locate the cancel flow at all -- but finding the cancel flow when it is deliberately buried in navigation, or requires a phone call (no DOM at all), is exactly where semantic/navigational reasoning becomes unavoidable.
- Agent-transfer note: not directly tested by name in the two agent papers reviewed; the general 41%/70%+ manipulation numbers are the closest hard evidence we have. DECEPTICON explicitly names "multi-step obstruction" as a case where "robust defenses... remain elusive." [DP-10]

**Confirmshaming**
- Structural mechanism: purely a copy/microcopy choice on the decline button/link (e.g. "No thanks, I don't want to save money") -- no distinctive DOM signature beyond being a button/link with unusually emotive text.
- Prevalence: not separately quantified with a prevalence percentage in sources reviewed; qualitatively documented as part of Brignull's original taxonomy and folded into Mathur et al.'s categories. [DP-06][DP-01]
- Detectability: requires semantic understanding. No structural signal distinguishes a confirmshaming decline link from a neutral one -- you need NLP/sentiment or LLM-based text classification. This is the clearest example in our set of a pattern that cannot be caught by geometry/DOM-state alone.
- Agent-transfer note: could plausibly affect agents less than humans (an LLM agent parsing "No thanks, I don't want to save money" as a decline action doesn't feel social shame) or more (an LLM might over-interpret the guilt-language semantically and second-guess a valid decline). No source tests this specific pattern in isolation for agents -- pure inference, flagged as such.

**Misdirection via visual hierarchy** (default button visually emphasized in color/size/position vs. a same-DOM-level alternative)
- Structural mechanism: implemented via CSS only -- the same underlying button elements, differing only in computed style (font-size, background-color, position/order).
- Prevalence: documented qualitatively across all taxonomies (Brignull, Gray, Mathur categorize this under "Interface Interference"/"Misdirection"); no isolated prevalence percentage found in sources reviewed. [DP-01][DP-03][DP-06]
- Detectability / agent-transfer note: the clearest case of the human/agent asymmetry the brief asks about. A DOM-reading or accessibility-tree-reading agent typically sees two semantically equivalent button elements and may not weight computed CSS salience the way a human's visual system does -- meaning this pattern may be genuinely invisible to a non-visual agent, the mirror image of clickjacking. An agent that also takes screenshots and does vision-based grounding would still be exposed. No source in our set specifically isolates this pattern's agent effect; this is inference, and it cuts the opposite direction from most other patterns in this table (helps rather than hurts a non-visual agent).

**Fake urgency / scarcity** ("Only 2 left!", countdown timers)
- Structural mechanism: timer countdowns are DOM/JS-driven (a setInterval decrementing a displayed value with no server-verified deadline); "X left in stock" is a text/number that may or may not correspond to a real inventory field.
- Prevalence / effect size: Luguri & Strahilevitz found scarcity/countdown manipulations had no significant effect on acceptance in their study (14.3%, approximately equal to control) -- an explicit negative/contradicting finding worth keeping. [DP-05] Legally, the EU DSA explicitly names fake countdown timers as a banned example. [DP-08] (unverified snippet) AidUI's detector reports its highest accuracy (F1 > 0.82) specifically on countdown/limited-time cues. [DP-13] (unverified, low confidence)
- Detectability: mostly structural for the countdown-timer variant (a setInterval-driven fake clock is detectable by re-checking the displayed deadline against wall-clock behavior, or noting it resets on reload); the "N items left" variant requires either a ground-truth inventory oracle (not available to a proxy) or heuristic suspicion of round/repeating numbers -- a weaker signal.
- Agent-transfer note: no direct agent-transfer study found for scarcity/urgency specifically. Given Luguri & Strahilevitz's finding that this pattern is weak even on humans, and no contrary agent evidence, we should not over-prioritize this pattern for the MCP proxy relative to overlays/pre-checked-boxes, where evidence of harm (on humans) and plausible agent-specific amplification (by reasoning) are both stronger.

## Automated detection landscape (signal used, reported accuracy)

- Mathur et al. crawler classifier: input is page text (scraped, not rendered), fed to a classifier driving a Selenium crawl. Reported accuracy ~83% (5-fold CV). [DP-12] (unverified secondary figure, medium confidence) Purely textual/structural pipeline -- closest in spirit to what a DOM/text-reading MCP proxy could replicate cheaply.
- AidUI (ICSE 2023): input is a screenshot (computer vision + NLP on rendered pixels). Overall P/R/F1 ~= 0.66/0.67/0.65; up to >0.82 F1 for visually cue-rich patterns (countdowns, bargain messages). [DP-13] (unverified, low confidence -- not directly fetched, author list unconfirmed). Needs a render step; strongest exactly on the patterns that are weakest on humans per Luguri & Strahilevitz (urgency/scarcity) -- a useful mismatch to note.
- UIGuard: input is a screenshot (CV) across 14 dark-pattern subtypes, 1,660 annotated screens. Macro/micro P/R/F1 ~= 0.82-0.83 / 0.77-0.82 / 0.79-0.82. [DP-14] (unverified, low confidence -- not directly fetched, author list unconfirmed). Reported as an improvement over AidUI; both are mobile/app-screen focused in the material surfaced, so applicability to arbitrary web DOMs is not confirmed.

Gap explicitly noted: none of the three detectors above were built for, or evaluated against, an agent-facing proxy use case (i.e. classifying a DOM/AX-tree snapshot the way an agent would consume it, rather than a human-facing screenshot or scraped marketing copy). This is a real gap in the literature, not something to paper over.

## Direct answer to the critical angle (agent transfer)

- Confirmed by direct measurement, not inference: dark patterns transfer to LLM web agents, at rates equal to or higher than their effect on humans (70%+ vs. 31% human baseline in DECEPTICON; ~41% average single-pattern susceptibility in Ersoy et al.), and -- counter to a natural assumption -- larger/more-reasoning models are more susceptible, not less. Prompt-level and guardrail-model defenses tested so far do not fix this. [DP-10][DP-11]
- Not yet directly measured, and should be labeled as our inference: which specific pattern types drive that aggregate number, and in particular whether invisible-to-agent patterns (visual-hierarchy misdirection) really do fail to transfer while invisible-to-human patterns (overlay clickjacking, pre-checked state) transfer more strongly to agents than to humans. Neither DECEPTICON nor Ersoy et al.'s abstracts/summaries fetched here break results out per-pattern-type with that granularity; a follow-up read of their full results tables (not just abstracts) would be needed to turn this from inference into a cited finding.
- Recommendation for the proxy: prioritize detection of the structurally detectable patterns above (overlay/clickjacking, pre-checked form state, DOM-level hidden-cost injection, cancel-flow step-count asymmetry) since (a) they need no semantic/NLP layer, (b) the literature's own detectors already show text/DOM-only classifiers can hit ~83% accuracy without any rendering step, and (c) the two agent-specific papers give us a credible existing benchmark environment (DECEPTICON, and Ersoy et al.'s LiteAgent/TrickyArena) against which to evaluate any defense, rather than inventing our own eval harness.

## Explicit disagreements / things the literature contradicts our intuition on

- We might assume fake urgency/scarcity is a top-tier dark pattern to defend against. Luguri & Strahilevitz's controlled experiment found it had no significant effect on human behavior, contradicting the intuition that countdown timers are highly effective -- the literature says this pattern is overrated relative to hidden-information and obstruction patterns, which had much larger measured effects (14.8% to 30.1%, and to 23.6%, respectively, vs. 14.3% ~= control for scarcity). [DP-05]
- We might assume better/larger models with more reasoning would be more robust to manipulation. DECEPTICON found the opposite: susceptibility increases with model size and test-time reasoning. [DP-10] This directly undercuts a "just use a smarter model" mitigation strategy for our proxy design.

## Known gaps / things not covered

- We did not find a large-scale (thousands-of-sites) crawl that measures dark patterns specifically against an agent's DOM/AX-tree consumption the way Mathur et al. did for human-facing pages -- this measurement gap is the field's most obvious open problem for our use case.
- Per-pattern-type breakdown of agent susceptibility (rather than aggregate 41%/70% figures) was not available from the abstract-level material fetched in this session; obtaining it would require reading the full results sections/tables of DECEPTICON (arXiv:2512.22894) and Ersoy et al. (arXiv:2510.18113) rather than their abstracts.
- Several taxonomy/regulatory claims (Gray CHI 2023 ontology detail, Brignull's exact 12-item list, the FTC report's exact four-category wording, DSA Article 25's exact clause text, Nouwens et al.'s exact prevalence percentage) rest on WebSearch snippets rather than a directly fetched and re-read primary document in this session, and are marked "unverified" with "medium" confidence in sources.json accordingly. They should be treated as very likely true and findable, but not as courtroom-ready citations until re-fetched directly.
